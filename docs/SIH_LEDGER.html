What the problem statement asks, clause by clause

The statement is one sentence: “Develop an AI/ML-enabled decision support platform capable of forecasting Antarctic sea-ice concentration, predicting iceberg trajectories, and identifying safe and fuel-efficient navigation routes for research vessels using satellite, oceanographic and meteorological datasets.” Read strictly, it contains five obligations. We meet three, partly meet one, and cannot presently meet one.

ClauseStatusEvidence
Forecasting Antarctic sea-ice concentration Measured Beats persistence at every lead 1–7 days: +18.5% at day 1 rising to +30.6% at day 7. Test set scored exactly once, split by time.
Predicting iceberg trajectories Designed Wagner (2017) closed-form drift implemented, reproduces the published coefficient table and its own 765 m critical length, 23/23 tests pass. Stepping it forward in time needs an ocean-current field we have not yet wired.
Identifying safe navigation routes Measured Real route computed on real satellite ice with PolarRoute 1.1.11: 8.6 days steaming, Cape Town → Bharati. Safety currently means a flat 80% ice cutoff, not a POLARIS index.
… and fuel-efficient Open We cannot honour this word today. PolarRoute’s fuel polynomial is fitted to a different hull, so we decline to quote any absolute figure. This needs a decision, not silence — see the callout below.
Using satellite, oceanographic and meteorological datasets Measured 1,096 real daily NOAA/NSIDC files, 0 failures, plus CMEMS GLORYS12. No synthetic data anywhere in the product.
Scope fidelity. The system deliberately does nothing the statement does not ask for.

The fuel clause needs a decision before the deck is written

Two honest options. Calibrate — fit a resistance curve for this hull from the shaft-power method already published for SA Agulhas II, which would let us quote fuel properly. Or report relatively — give fuel only as a ratio between candidate routes and state plainly that the absolute figure belongs to another vessel. The second is achievable now and is what the code already does; the first is the better answer if there is time. What is not acceptable is quoting a number the hull does not own.

01

The solution, in the three decisions that shape it

One — we correct the operational forecast rather than compete with it

CMEMS already forecasts Antarctic sea ice ten days ahead while assimilating live satellite data. A team training a U-Net to beat it from scratch is picking a fight with an assimilating physical model. We predict the correction instead:

corrected_SIC = clip( CMEMS_forecast + model_Δ , 0 , 1 )

The prediction head is zero-initialised, so an untrained or failing model contributes exactly nothing and the output is the physics forecast. The floor is CMEMS’s skill, not zero. That is a genuine safety property: model failure degrades to the baseline rather than falling off a cliff.

Two — ashore and aboard are different machines, because the link says so

Geostationary VSAT does not reach south of about 70°S. Polar comms are Iridium-class, 704 kbps at absolute best and usually far less. So: everything needing bandwidth or a GPU stays ashore; everything needing to answer a question runs aboard; what crosses the link is a compressed environmental state, never an answer.

This is not our invention. The Canadian Coast Guard’s Ice Navigation in Canadian Waters already separates a strategic phase ashore from a tactical phase on the bridge, and leaves the routing recommendation with the shore ice desk while the bridge keeps real-time deviation authority. We arrived at the same split from bandwidth arithmetic and then found the doctrine.

Three — the master decides, and the system argues only once

We never open by drawing our line over theirs. The system evaluates the master’s intended route first — transit distribution, worst ice, where the risk sits — then offers alternatives as annotated deltas with reasons and triggers. At departure it argues its case. Once the master chooses, that route becomes the route and the system stops re-litigating it, switching to micro-adjustments and hazards he might miss.

A route we produce that the master rejects for good reasons is a successful interaction. The failure mode we care about is an unexplained recommendation, not a rejected one — and a system that keeps arguing for a rejected route is a system that gets switched off.

02

Innovation and uniqueness

Uniqueness is only meaningful against what everyone else will build. For an MoES data problem, convergence is documented: the SIH 2025 FloatChat statement produced at least four independent public implementations of the same pattern — a chatbot over real NetCDF, with retrieval standing in for the model. The predictable shape here is CMEMS plus a U-Net plus A* plus a Leaflet dashboard.

We checked whether the customer already has this. They do not.

Claiming novelty without checking the ministry’s own tooling is how a pitch dies in the first question. We manually reviewed npdc.ncpor.res.in, NCPOR’s “Research Vessel Movements” pages and data.ncpor.res.in. What exists is static archival material — PDF cruise schedules and historical reports for ORV Sagar Kanya, a different vessel — plus station observations from the Maitri and Bharati weather systems and generic catalogue and plotting tools. No sea-ice forecast, no iceberg tracking, no routing tool anywhere. This is greenfield, and we can say so having looked.

The one that is hardest to copy

Ordering quality control before fusion. A fusion layer without QC cannot tell a sensor artifact from genuine inter-source disagreement, and will widen a route around a phantom. We found this the expensive way: the NOAA/NSIDC record writes suppressed coastal pixels as 0.0, inside valid_range [0,100], so any unguarded reader accepts them as open water. It fires on 100% of days. Four of the eight December 2019 days on which Bharati looked reachable were this artifact.

No LLM, no RAG, no agent framework appears anywhere in this system

There is no natural-language task in the problem statement. Every component is a numerical prediction or a graph search, for which deterministic and classical methods are cheaper here, verifiable against held-out data, and able to run offline on a ship’s bridge. That last property is not a preference — it is the deployment constraint.

04

How the system knows it is degraded

This is the mechanism judges will probe, because it is where most decision-support systems are hollow. It runs at three timescales, and they are genuinely separate things.

Per cell, at ingest100% of days the land-spillover filter fires; mean 118 cells, max 488
Bharati 200 km box43.2% of days across 2018–2020 carry suspect cells
Between sources4 independent SIC products; disagreement widens the routed bound
Over time1 day yesterday’s forecast against today’s observation, free and daily at sea

What makes it more than a banner is a pre-committed number. SIC_upper claims 90% coverage and must measure between 85% and 96% on held-out marginal-ice-zone cells at lead 5. Over-coverage is also failure: a bound right 99% of the time is too wide to change a route. If it misses, the response is already written — the bound stops being the routed field, the confidence slider is removed from the interface rather than left as theatre, and the claim is publicly demoted. That branch was written before the numbers existed, so it cannot be renegotiated after them.

The degradation ladder — every rung always available, and announced

1
Corrected forecast plus calibrated interval Normal operation. Full route line, waypoints, iceberg exclusion polygons.
2
Raw CMEMS, labelled uncorrected Our model failed its daily verification. Band from measured CMEMS-versus-observation error.
3
Persistence from the newest observation Forecast feed lost. Lighter line, waypoints indicative only.
4
Climatology for the date No recent observation at all. Dashed corridor, not a route: “which way to go, not where to steer.”

Falling to rung 3 or 4 widens the corridor and moves decision points earlier — less information means committing later, not sailing the same line with less confidence. The climatology uses only the last ten years, because a 1979-based climatology would be biased high against the documented post-2016 Antarctic regime shift.

Two results that came out against us, kept in the record

Daily re-planning did not beat static planning. On the one departure date tested, regret was 0.03 days and the daily arm was slower, because re-planning from the ship’s live position takes locally-optimal turns a single global optimisation avoids. One date is one sample and the sweep is not yet run — but we report the null.

We withdrew a headline number. An earlier 17.4-day transit was computed with the beam and top speed both wrong; corrected, it is 8.6 days. Specification errors dominated model errors at that stage. And 8.6 days is steaming time only — no sea state, no bathymetry, no cargo operations. It is a lower bound, not a voyage.

06

Viability — it has to work at 70°S

Viability for this system means one thing: does it still function when the link is gone? The bandwidth budget is therefore a build-breaking constraint rather than a documented aspiration.

PayloadSizeStatus

 Leading with the route polyline would be quoting the smallest payload in the system as if it were representative. The mesh is the honest unit.
  • The budget is written down as a build-breaking rule (ADR-017) — but the test that would enforce it is not written yet, and the daily delta has never been measured. Until both exist this is an intention, not a constraint, and we say so.
  • If measurement disappoints, the budget still does not move. The pre-committed response is to coarsen the open-ocean mesh or stretch the refresh to two or three days.
  • The ship re-corrects the whole ten-day packed forecast aboard from one fresh corridor observation, roughly 5–15 KB. A packed third-party forecast is frozen at build time and cannot do this at any bandwidth.
  • The demo is unfakeable: disconnect the network mid-presentation and keep re-planning. The demo setup is the product.

08

The strategy, which is the same in every case

Write the response before the result. Every mitigation in the table above was committed to in writing while the outcome was still unknown, which is what stops a threshold from quietly moving once the numbers arrive. Four worked examples:

  • The conformal gate names its own failure and its own consequence, including the upper bound — over-coverage is failure too, because a bound that is right 99% of the time cannot change a route.
  • The bandwidth budget names its own response in advance — coarsen the mesh, never move the number. The enforcing test is still owed, and that gap is on the backlog rather than glossed.
  • The iceberg buffer auto-downgrades from hard exclusion to weighted cost if it would blockade more than 5% of the navigable corridor, and says so — a safety layer that closes the sea is not a safety layer.
  • The drift model falls back to pure physics when the learned correction goes out of distribution, with the trigger unit-tested to actually fire.

And the ordering rule for anything new

Reuse, then adapt, then extend, then wrap, then replace — in that order. PolarRoute and meshiphi are extended through their own documented hooks and never forked, which is why an upstream fix reaches us for free and why the routing engine is not ours to get wrong.

09

Impact, stated in the terms the operation uses

The measured result reframed what this system is for, and it is worth being precise about, because it is not the answer most teams will give.

The ocean is not the problem. The last hundred kilometres is.

For this vessel at an 80% ice limit through December 2019, Bharati’s own cell was closed on 23 of 31 days while the approach point 100 km north was open on 31 of 31. The open-ocean leg — about 5,800 km of a 5,813 km route — carries mean 6% ice. There is almost nothing to optimise out there.

So the question this system exists to answer is not “which line do we draw across the Southern Ocean.” It is: when should we arrive, will the last 100 km be open when we get there, how long will it stay open, and if it is shut — do we wait, or switch to helicopter and barge? That is a forecasting question with a horizon of days, which is exactly the horizon our model addresses.

  • It matches how Indian resupply actually works. The vessel holds at the fast-ice edge and moves cargo the remaining distance by air and barge, which is why Golovnin carries two helicopters. Timing that decision is worth days of station time.
  • It removes a class of confident wrong answer. A planner reading the raw record would have called Bharati reachable on four December days when the satellite had simply stopped looking.
  • It is honest about a hazard it cannot see. The smallest iceberg any Antarctic-wide product tracks is roughly 800 times larger than a growler. We say so rather than implying a warning we cannot give — and instead forecast when the lookout will be blind, which we can.
  • It is reusable beyond this ship. The vessel, corridor and season are configuration, so the same system serves a different charter or a different station without a rewrite.

What this system will never claim

Declaring the boundary is not a weakness in a safety context; it is the part that makes the rest believable. An examiner who finds an undeclared limit stops trusting the declared numbers.
