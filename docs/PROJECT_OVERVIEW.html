§1Read this first — the project in plain English

Written for someone who has just joined the team and knows nothing about sea ice, ships, or this problem statement. Nothing here requires prior knowledge.

1.1 The situation

  1. Antarctica is surrounded by sea ice — a floating layer of frozen seawater that forms, drifts, cracks and melts. It is not fixed. It can move tens of kilometres in a day during a storm.
  2. India operates two permanent research stations in Antarctica: Maitri (Schirmacher Oasis, 70.8°S 11.7°E) and Bharati (Larsemann Hills, 69.4°S 76.2°E). A third, Maitri-II, is planned.
  3. Once a year, during the austral summer (roughly December to April), a ship must deliver fuel, food, equipment and personnel to those stations. This is the Indian Scientific Expedition to Antarctica (ISEA), run by NCPOR under the Ministry of Earth Sciences.
  4. The ship currently used is the MV Vasiliy Golovnin, a chartered ice-strengthened Russian diesel-electric vessel. Expeditions are flagged off from Goa, but personnel fly to Cape Town, and the sea leg runs Cape Town → Bharati → Maitri.
  5. If the ship picks a bad path, it can become beset — stuck in ice. That costs days or weeks, burns fuel, risks the hull, and can mean a station does not get resupplied before the window closes.

1.2 The problem we are solving

The problem in one sentence

A ship's captain needs to know what the ice will look like days from now, where the icebergs will have drifted to, and which route through all of that is both safe and fuel-efficient — and they need to know it on a ship with almost no internet connection.

Each part of that sentence is a hard problem on its own:

  • Forecasting the ice. Physics supercomputers already forecast sea ice, but their forecasts are systematically wrong in predictable places — especially at the ice edge, which is exactly where ships sail.
  • Predicting icebergs. Icebergs drift under wind, current and the Earth's rotation. Their positions are only observed weekly, and their future positions carry large, growing uncertainty.
  • Finding a route. The best route is not the shortest. It trades distance against ice, weather, water depth and fuel, and the constraints themselves are uncertain.
  • Doing it offline. Geostationary satellite internet does not reliably cover south of about 70°S. Polar ships use Iridium, where the practical budget for a routine message is well under 50 kilobytes.

1.3 What we are building

A decision-support platform — not an autopilot. It advises; the master decides. It has two halves that are the same codebase running in two modes:

  1. The shore tier runs at NCPOR on a normal workstation. It has bandwidth and a GPU. It downloads satellite and forecast data, runs the AI models, and every night builds a small compressed file called a voyage pack.
  2. The vessel tier runs on a laptop on the ship's bridge. It receives the voyage pack over Iridium — or on a USB stick at port — and then works with no internet at all. It can recompute routes on demand in about nine seconds.
The design rule that follows from this

Ashore: everything that needs bandwidth or a GPU. Aboard: everything that needs to answer a question. Sync: a compressed picture of the environment, never a finished answer. We deliberately do not send the ship a finished route, because then the captain could not ask "what if I go further east?" — and a system that cannot answer the operator's next question is a picture, not a tool.

1.4 The one idea at the heart of the AI

This is the single most important thing to understand about the project.

  1. There is already a good physics forecast of sea ice, produced by the European Copernicus Marine Service (CMEMS). It runs on supercomputers and assimilates live satellite data. We could never out-build it.
  2. So we do not compete with it. We correct it. Our neural network does not predict sea ice. It predicts how wrong the physics forecast is going to be, and we add that correction on top.
corrected sea-ice concentration  =  clip( physics forecast  +  our predicted correction ,  0 , 1 )
  • Because the network outputs a correction, an untrained network outputs exactly zero and reproduces the physics forecast unchanged. Our floor is the physics model's skill, not zero. We can only be judged on what we add.
  • The network is told, per grid cell, how wrong the forecast is right now — the difference between the forecast and the most recent real satellite observation. This is called the innovation channel, and it is what makes the whole thing work.
Why this matters — a real lesson from our own history

The first version of our model saw only the physics forecast, with no satellite observations. It scored RMSE 0.1051 and lost badly to the dumbest possible baseline ("assume tomorrow's ice looks like today's"). Giving the model access to what the satellite actually saw is what closed the gap. We found this by measuring it ourselves, before a judge could.

1.5 What we have achieved so far

1.6 What we are not claiming

  • We are not replacing electronic navigational charts. This is decision support.
  • We do not predict growlers and bergy bits — the small ice that actually holes a hull. No dataset tracks them.
  • We do not print confident absolute fuel numbers, because the fuel curve we have is calibrated for a different ship.
  • We do not claim immunity to climate change. We claim the system degrades visibly rather than silently.

2.5 What happens when things go wrong

The system is designed around graceful degradation. It never goes silent.

FailureBehaviour
No connectivity for daysNormal operation on the cached pack; staleness escalates green → amber → red; past pack validity it enters climatology mode — a dashed planning corridor, not a route line, with a permanent banner. Never silence.
Forecast lead exceeds validated skillTier down, do not refuse. Forecast-grade → CMEMS-direct → climatology-grade. The plan always covers the whole voyage; the grade of each leg is visible in the UI.
Path smoothing fails or hangsServe the unsmoothed Dijkstra path (0.7 s, verified) flagged unsmoothed. The fallback is inherent to the pipeline — no extra code.

Ensemble members disagree stronglyThe bound widens → automatically more cautious route + explicit low-confidence banner.

A data source is down at build timePer-channel imputation policy; if the base forecast or the observations are missing, an obs-only pack ships instead — a declared pack type, not an error state.

4.2 The one genuine data gap

There is no “Antarctic shipping lane” dataset — and there never was

No downloadable recommended-ice-lane dataset exists from the IHO or anyone else. Official Electronic Navigational Charts for Antarctic waters are licensed and paywalled through national hydrographic offices, and Antarctic coverage is notoriously sparse because the region is barely surveyed. The IMO Polar Code and METAREA products are safety-broadcast systems, not structured route datasets.

This is not a failure in our research — it is a confirmation that route computation is the product's actual job, not an input it should be looking to ingest.

4.3 A related honesty note on bathymetry

Much of the Antarctic corridor's GEBCO bathymetry is predicted from satellite altimetry, not surveyed by echo sounder. A router that treats depth as truth is trusting a model with metre-to-hundreds-of-metre error. We handle this rather than merely admitting it:

  • We ingest GEBCO's Type Identifier (TID) grid and carry it as a mesh layer.
  • The depth margin is conditional on provenance: minimum under-keel clearance of 20 m in surveyed cells and max(50 m, 3 × draft) in predicted cells.
  • A “predicted bathymetry” overlay shows the master which parts of a route cross unsurveyed seabed, and the disclosure sits next to the depth legend in the product — not only in a document.

5.1 Model 1 — Sea-ice concentration bias-correction residual U-Net trained & measured

This is the load-bearing AI of the whole system. Everything else consumes its output.

What it does

It reads a physics sea-ice forecast plus the last week of real satellite observations, and outputs a correction field — a per-grid-cell number that gets added to the forecast.

ΔSIC_k  =  g_θ( F_k ,  O_{t-6..t} ,  F_0 − O_t ,  forcing ,  k )
SIC_k   =  clip( F_k + ΔSIC_k ,  0 ,  1 )

  F_k        the physics forecast valid k days ahead
  O_{t-6..t} the last 7 days of real satellite observations
  F_0 − O_t  the INNOVATION — how wrong the forecast is right now, at this cell
  k          the lead time, fed in as its own input channel

Why a residual, and not a direct forecast

  1. The floor is CMEMS's skill, not zero. ΔSIC = 0 reproduces the operational forecast exactly. The model cannot be much worse than the bar, and can only be judged on what it adds.
  2. The innovation channel targets a documented, specific defect. Copernicus is known to systematically underestimate ice concentration in the marginal ice zone. Anchoring the correction to the current per-cell error is the mechanism by which the model can learn that specific bias rather than a generic climatological offset.
  3. The model can be small. A residual on an already-skilful field needs far less capacity than a forecaster — ~3 M parameters instead of 8–15 M, which is what makes the whole ensemble fit inside free-tier GPU hours.
This was a corrected mistake, and the correction is instructive

Revision 1 of the design committed to a from-scratch direct forecaster competing with CMEMS. An adversarial review killed it: our own competitive analysis had already called CMEMS “the operational baseline any novel forecasting claim has to beat, and it's a hard bar,” and then we set our bar at naive persistence instead. We had ducked our own bar. The gap analysis had even named the right method — “DL bias-correction on top of a short-range ice-ocean model” — but the example given was TOPAZ4, an Arctic system, so it was filed as unusable. That was reading a method as a product. The method transfers to Antarctica; the global CMEMS system was already sitting in our own data inventory, being used for currents and ignored for ice.

Output headZero-initialised (nn.init.zeros_ on both weight and bias). An untrained model is therefore a strict no-op that reproduces the forecast exactly, rather than injecting random noise into an already-skilful field.
Grid handlingThe 332×316 polar-stereographic grid is not divisible by 2³, so the input is replicate-padded up to a multiple and cropped back at the end, keeping skip connections aligned.

Lead handlingLead time is an input channel, not an output head. One network serves all leads 1–10, and the training set is 10× larger per forecast cycle.

How it is evaluated — and the gate that can kill it

  • Strictly temporal split. Never random. Sea ice barely changes overnight, so a random split puts adjacent days on both sides and produces a spectacular, meaningless score. This is the most common fatal flaw in ML-on-climate submissions and is stated explicitly for that reason.
  • Baseline 1 — raw packed CMEMS. Mandatory and build-blocking. For each lead, the corrected forecast must beat the raw forecast on both IIEE (integrated ice-edge error) and CRPS, on held-out real forecast cycles. A lead that fails is not claimed — the pack ships the raw field for that lead labelled uncorrected, and the declared skill horizon shortens.
  • Baseline 2 — damped anomaly persistence as the floor.
  • Baseline 3 — the trivial uncertainty proxy. The calibrated bound must beat free inter-source disagreement as an uncertainty estimator. If a three-way difference predicts the error as well as the trained σ does, the trained σ is decorative and we say so.
  • Metrics: RMSE, IIEE at the 15 % threshold (the navigationally meaningful one), binary F1 at the vessel-relevant threshold, CRPS, interval score.
  • Stress test: 2023, the Antarctic extreme-minimum year, evaluated and reported separately, with no threshold — publication is the requirement, not passing.

5.2 Model 2 — Iceberg drift, Wagner (2017) closed form designed

Load-bearing, and deliberately not machine learning.

v_i  =  v_w  +  γ( α · k̂ × v_a  +  β · v_a )

  v_i  iceberg velocity        v_w  ocean current velocity
  v_a  wind velocity           k̂    vertical unit vector (Coriolis term)
  α, β, γ  functions of berg dimensions and latitude
  • Inputs: ocean current (CMEMS), wind (ERA5 historical / GFS operational), berg length and width (BYU/USNIC), latitude (Coriolis).
  • No fitted parameters beyond standard literature drag coefficients.
  • Why it is the baseline: zero training data required, closed-form (so no ODE integration and therefore no numerical-stability failure mid-demo), implements in ~100 lines, unit-testable against the published reference, and it is the physics core that IDRIFTNET — the 2025 state of the art — itself builds on. It is not a strawman baseline; it is the field's base case.
  • Evaluation: leave-one-iceberg-out. Segments from the same berg are heavily correlated, so a random segment split leaks the same trajectory into both sides and is meaningless. Metrics are Average and Final Displacement Error at 24 / 48 / 72 h.
The horizon cap, and why it exists

Physics-only displacement error is 127–147 km at 7 days. An honest uncertainty radius that size around every tracked berg turns the hazard layer into either an ocean-wide blockade (honest but useless) or a quietly shrunk buffer (useful but dishonest). Neither is acceptable. So: drift is projected 72 hours, never further. Beyond that we draw nothing and show last-known positions labelled “position as of <date>, not projected” — a stale fact, not a fabricated forecast. And if exclusion polygons ever cover more than 5 % of navigable corridor area, the layer auto-downgrades from hard exclusion to a weighted cost, and the UI says so.

5.3 Model 3 — Iceberg drift residual, gradient boosting stretch goal

  • What it does: predicts the Wagner model's 24-hour displacement residual (Δx, Δy) from tabular features — berg major/minor axis, local wind speed and direction, current speed and direction, local ice concentration, latitude, distance from coast.
  • Why gradient boosting and not a neural network: the residual problem is small-data and tabular — BYU/USNIC give weekly-to-daily positions for large tabular bergs only. Gradient boosting is the correct tool for that regime. IDRIFTNET's own stated motivation for going hybrid was that pure neural approaches converge poorly on the available data; the same logic pushes further toward classical ML for the correction term.
  • Interpretability is a judging asset here. Feature importances let us demonstrate that the model recovers known physics — wind dominating for smaller bergs, currents dominating for large tabular ones. A model that rediscovers the physics is far more convincing than one that merely scores well.
  • It must beat both baselines to ship — velocity persistence and pure Wagner. If it does not, the physics baseline ships alone, and that is a perfectly good outcome.

The fallback is specified as a rule, not an intention, and is unit-tested:

fire_fallback(x, Δ)  =  ANY_OOD(x)  OR  |Δ| > c_sanity

ANY_OOD(x) : for any feature j,  x_j < min_j − 0.10·range_j
                              or x_j > max_j + 0.10·range_j
             (ranges taken from the TRAINING fold only, frozen at fit time
              and serialised into the model artefact next to the weights)
c_sanity   : p99 of |residual correction| over the training fold, in km

Per-feature ranges beat Mahalanobis distance or an isolation forest here because they are inspectable in the UI (“berg B-15 fell outside the trained wind-speed range”) and cannot themselves be miscalibrated. Two unit tests assert the fallback actually fires — because an untested fallback is an unfired fallback.

Why we will not print an absolute fuel number

PolarRoute's fuel polynomial is empirically fitted to the RRS Sir David Attenborough, a British ship. We do not have the Vasiliy Golovnin's power/fuel curve. Therefore fuel is reported as a relative comparison between candidate routes — which is what actually informs the decision — and any absolute tonnes/day figure is labelled uncalibrated model output. Competitors will display confident absolute fuel savings they cannot possibly have calibrated. Declining to do so is a differentiator, not a shortfall.

Similarly, ice thickness and density come from meshiphi's lookup table, whose own docstring reads “Creates a simulated dataset … based on scientific literature.” It is a literature-derived climatological parameterisation of the kind used throughout the field — not fabricated data, but it must be labelled, because the ice-resistance figure inherits its uncertainty. Note also that without thickness and density present in the mesh, the ice-resistance speed adjustment silently does nothing — verified in source, and a live trap for anyone wiring this up.

5.5 Model 5 — Route optimiser run on real data

  • Algorithm: Dijkstra over the non-uniform adaptive mesh — exact on that mesh, not a heuristic — followed by Newton-method crossing smoothing, which relaxes cell-boundary crossing points the way light refracts, turning a kinked shortest path into a physically sailable one.
  • Objectives available: traveltime, fuel, battery.
  • Multi-objective handling. PolarRoute accepts only a single objective string, so we sweep: 2 objectives × 6 risk-tolerance settings = 12 runs, ~7 s each ≈ 84 s. Every resulting route is then scored on all three quantities — passage time, modelled fuel, and route-integrated ice risk — so the trade-off triple is measured on the route, not inherited from the objective it was solved under. Dominated routes are discarded.
  • Named honestly: we call it a dominance-filtered candidate set (12-run sweep), not a “Pareto frontier” — 12 samples of a continuous trade-off surface is a set, not a frontier. If the filter leaves one route, we show one route and say the trade-off collapsed.

5.6 The two supporting layers

Conformal calibration

score  s_i  =  ( y_i − μ_i ) / ( σ_i + ε )              one-sided: we bound only from above
q̂(stratum) =  ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉ / n  empirical quantile of { s_i } within the stratum
SIC_upper  =  clip( μ + q̂(lead, regime) · σ , 0 , 1 )
  • Distribution-free — makes no Gaussian assumption about a variable bounded in [0,1] whose error at the ice edge is strongly asymmetric.
  • Stratified into 30 strata: 10 lead times × 3 regimes (open water <15 %, marginal ice zone 15–80 %, pack >80 %). Miscalibration is worst in the MIZ at long leads — exactly where the router consumes the bound.
  • The operator control is target coverage (80/90/95 %), not a sigma multiple. The demo line changes from “this is 1.28 sigma” to “this says 90 % and it measured 90.4 % on held-out data.”
  • The ensemble supplies the spatial shape of the uncertainty; the conformal quantile supplies its magnitude, from measured residuals.
Pre-committed pass/fail — written before the numbers exist

At nominal 90 % coverage, on held-out 2022–24 data: empirical coverage in MIZ cells at lead 5 must fall in [85 %, 96 %] — over-coverage fails too, because a bound that is right 99 % of the time is too wide to steer by — and mean bound width at lead 5 must be under 25 SIC-%. If any check fails, the bound stops being the routed field, the slider is removed from the UI rather than left as theatre, and the corresponding claim is publicly demoted. This branch is written down now so it cannot be quietly renegotiated afterwards.

Multi-source fusion and disagreement

  • Where two or more independent, QC-clean sources cover a cell, inter-source disagreement is retained as its own mesh layer.
  • Disagreement widens the uncertainty bound, so the router is automatically most cautious exactly where the observational record is least trustworthy. It changes the route — it is not a second basemap.
  • QC runs first. Without it, a fusion layer cannot tell a sensor artifact from genuine disagreement, and would widen the route around a phantom. Flagged cells are excluded from the disagreement statistic and rendered distinctly — they are different facts.

6.3 Caveats attached to these results — state them, do not hide them

  1. The background is a reanalysis, not a forecast — the sharpest question we will be asked. Raw GLORYS12 scores 0.1630 at lead 1 and 0.1637 at lead 7 — essentially flat. A real forecast degrades with lead time; this does not, because a reanalysis assimilates satellite observations including near the date being predicted. The honest answer: if GLORYS12 contained the answer its own RMSE would be near zero rather than 0.163, which is worse than persistence at every horizon — so it is not handing over the target. But it may carry some future signal, and we do not claim otherwise. The clean test is the ablation below, and production corrects a real forecast where this cannot arise.
  2. Validation scores better than test at every lead (lead 3: val 0.0611 vs test 0.0720). Expected — validation selects the epoch, so it is optimistically biased. Always quote the test numbers.
  3. Single model per horizon, no uncertainty layer yet. Production trains five and turns their disagreement into a calibrated safety margin that changes the route.
  4. 12 channels, not 19. No ERA5 wind/temperature, no OISST — the physical drivers of ice motion are currently invisible to the model.
  5. ~2 years of training data (2019–2020) versus 1993–2024 for production.
  6. Corridor region only (5°W–85°E, 48°S–80°S), not circumpolar.
  7. Routing has no bathymetry. GEBCO was not downloaded; the mesh reported “no elevation data” for every cell. Shallow water and land are not being avoided. This must be added before any claim about navigational safety.
  8. Routing used observed ice, not forecast ice. Wiring the trained model into the router is what turns this from a hindsight map into a decision-support tool.
The ablation that settles caveat 1 not yet run

Retrain with the background channel zeroed, leaving only real past observations and derived channels. If the score barely moves — the background contributes little, leakage is a non-issue, and the model's skill comes from observation history. Strongest possible answer. If the score drops sharply — the background is doing real work, and we must state that the prototype's numbers may not transfer to a live-forecast setting until production tests it. Either outcome is worth having before a viva. Cost: about 20 minutes.

§7What makes this different from the obvious build

Given only a one-sentence problem statement, every competent team converges on roughly the same architecture. Knowing what that is — and where it is shallow — is how we differ.

7.1 What a strong competing team plausibly builds

  1. Pull CMEMS ice concentration + NIC/BYU iceberg positions + ERA5 wind/current data.
  2. Fine-tune or reimplement a U-Net for short-horizon ice forecasting.
  3. Iceberg trajectory as either pure physics or an LSTM — rarely both.
  4. Routing as A*/Dijkstra with ice as an edge-cost penalty — a static shortest path.
  5. Wrap it in a Leaflet dashboard with a chat layer, demo three cherry-picked scenarios.

We know this is the convergent pattern because it already happened: for a sibling MoES problem statement in SIH 2025 (“FloatChat”), at least four teams independently built the same chatbot-over-real-data wrapper, where the “AI” was retrieval and NL-to-SQL translation rather than a trained predictive model.

7.2 The seven differentiators

D1 — Uncertainty reaches the routing decision, not just the legend
We route on a probabilistic upper bound of ice concentration, not a point forecast. Verified gap in the incumbent: meshiphi's own IceNet loader ingests forecasts and explicitly discards the uncertainty — its df.drop(columns=[...]) call removes sic_stddev and ensemble_members. BAS's own production polar router plans on the mean and throws the spread away. We reinstate it, and our vessel subclass thresholds on the calibrated bound instead of the raw field. Falsifiable: moving the risk slider must produce a different route geometry, not different shading.
D2 — Physics-first drift with a classical-ML residual, and a stated blind spot
Neither an opaque black box nor physics-only. Honesty commitment displayed in the UI, not just the docs: BYU/USNIC track only large tabular bergs. Growlers and bergy bits — the ice that actually holes a hull — are neither in the training data nor predicted. Competitors will present iceberg avoidance as solved. It is not.
D3 — Multi-source disagreement surfaced and acted on, not resolved away
Grounded in a documented finding: Copernicus systematically underestimates ice concentration relative to regional charts, especially in the marginal ice zone. A team using one source as ground truth is building on a documented bias without saying so. Where our sources disagree, the system becomes more cautious automatically.
D4 — Bandwidth-honest offline-first design, enforced in CI
The tier boundary is set at “sync environmental state, not answers,” so the master can change waypoints and re-plan with zero connectivity. A CI test fails the build if a pack exceeds budget. Demo consequence: disconnect the network mid-demo and continue re-planning routes — unfakeable, and it is how the system is designed to work at sea.
D5 — Corridor- and season-specific, against the real vessel
Built for the actual Cape Town → Bharati/Maitri austral-summer run, not for “Antarctica” in general. Models still train circumpolar because more data is free for a fully convolutional network — only the operational claim is scoped. An MoES/NCPOR audience knows this corridor and this ship; demonstrated fidelity on the real case is verifiable, circumpolar generality is not.
D6 — Honest quantification, including where we decline to quantify
Fuel relative only. Ice thickness disclosed as a literature lookup table. Skill horizon is an output of the evaluation, not an assumption. Bathymetry disclosed as largely predicted. Iceberg drift capped at 72 h. Why this is differentiation and not weakness: competitors will display confident absolute fuel savings and a single authoritative ice number, and every one of those figures is undefendable under questioning. Being the team that already knows which of its numbers are soft is the stronger position — and StormGeo, the actual commercial incumbent, keeps human route analysts in the loop 24/7 precisely because full automation is not trusted at the edges.
D7 — Real prior art reused and extended, with the extensions named
Reuse → adapt → extend → wrap → replace was applied in order, and the reuse candidate survived. We use BAS's own tool and add three specific things it does not do: uncertainty-aware ice thresholding, an iceberg hazard layer through the native excluded_zones hook (no fork required — source-checked), and a dominance-filtered candidate route set. A domain-literate judge will know PolarRoute exists. Arriving with “we used BAS's tool and here are three things it does not do that we added” is far stronger than a from-scratch A* that invites the question of why the field's own tool was ignored.

8.2 The riskiest assumption in the whole design

Stated plainly

That the calibrated bound is well enough covered for routing on it to be genuinely safer, rather than merely more conservative-looking. The entire uncertainty-aware differentiation and the safety argument for the routing layer rest on it.

Conformal calibration's coverage guarantee assumes exchangeability between calibration residuals and operations — and the post-2016 Antarctic regime is precisely the distribution shift that can break exchangeability. Mitigated by a pre-committed numerical pass/fail rule with a written demotion branch (§5.6), a build-blocking baseline, acceptance on real forecast cycles rather than only the reanalysis proxy, and separate reporting on 2023.

8.3 Climate change is a live threat to this system, and here is the answer

Antarctic sea-ice extent has mostly sat below the 1981–2010 average since 2016, with record-low summers in 2022 and 2023 and continuing lows through 2025. The winter maximum hit a record low of 16.96 million km² on 10 September 2023. The published framing is a new sea-ice state which “exhibits different seasonal persistence characteristics, suggesting that the underlying processes controlling Antarctic sea ice coverage may have altered.”

Two consequences that matter:

  1. Persistence — our headline baseline — is itself regime-dependent. “Different seasonal persistence characteristics” means the +18–31 % is measured against a moving target. Gains must be reported per regime, never pooled across 2015 and 2023 as if they were the same ocean.
  2. It sharpens the exchangeability risk in §8.2 directly.

Five defences, in order of how much they actually buy:

  1. The architecture is already the main defence. We predict a correction to a physics model, not the ice. That model ingests today's assimilated ocean state, so the trend and the regime arrive through the background field, not from the network's memory. Our network only has to learn the model's systematic error pattern — a property of its physics, which drifts far more slowly than the climate.
  2. Train on anomalies against a trailing climatology, not raw concentration, so a shifting mean does not push inputs out of distribution.
  3. Deliberately hold out the broken years. Train excluding 2016 and 2023, then test on them. Surviving the two years that broke the field is evidence, not hope.
  4. Drift monitoring in production. Watch the innovation channel and the residual distribution; when they leave the calibration distribution, widen uncertainty and raise a flag rather than staying confident.
  5. Conformal prediction is the honest failure mode. When exchangeability breaks — which is what climate change does — coverage degrades visibly and measurably.
The position to state

“We do not claim immunity to climate change. We claim the system degrades loudly rather than silently.” For a safety system that is a stronger and far more credible position than claiming robustness.

10.4 New findings from the navigation review (1 September 2026)

A design review against the full problem-statement text surfaced three material gaps, all verified from primary sources or installed source code. Full detail in docs/NAVIGATION_RESEARCH.md.

(a) The vessel ice limit should be IMO POLARIS, not a number we chose

The prototype uses max_ice_conc: 80 — invented. The IMO already mandates a framework: MSC.1/Circ.1519, the Polar Operational Limit Assessment Risk Indexing System (POLARIS), referenced by the Polar Code.

RIO  =  (C₁ × RIV₁) + (C₂ × RIV₂) + … + (Cₙ × RIVₙ)

  Cᵢ   concentration of ice type i, in tenths
  RIVᵢ Risk Index Value for that ice type at THIS SHIP'S ice class

  RIO ≥ 0     normal operation
  −10 ≤ RIO < 0  elevated risk — speed capped (PC1 11 kt · PC2 8 kt · PC3–PC5 5 kt · below PC5 3 kt)
  RIO < −10   avoid in voyage planning
  • Turns an invented threshold into a number a judge can look up.
  • Makes the ship's ice class a real input that changes the answer — exactly what the problem statement asks for.
  • Makes risk continuous rather than binary, so the router can prefer safer ice instead of only avoiding a cliff edge.
  • §1.7.3 requires a safe stand-off distance from glacial ice — the iceberg safety buffer, as a regulatory requirement rather than an invented one.
  • §1.6.4 allows adding +10 to the RIO along an icebreaker's broken track — the formal mechanism by which a following vessel benefits from a leader, and the basis for any convoy or fleet-ordering model.
And it forces a model upgrade — in a good way

RIO is indexed by ice type, not concentration, and WMO stage-of-development maps to thickness (new <10 cm · grey 10–15 · grey-white 15–30 · thin first-year 30–70 · medium 70–120 · thick >120 cm). So POLARIS cannot be computed from a concentration forecast alone — it requires a thickness forecast too. GLORYS12 carries sithick, and the U-Net extends to a second output channel with no architectural change. This is not scope creep; it is a model requirement falling out of the regulation.

(b) PolarRoute's router has no time dimension — verified by source inspection

Searching the installed package for time-dependent mesh handling returns nothing in the route planner; datetime appears only as a running sum over output legs. Mesh time bounds select and aggregate a slice; they do not produce per-timestep layers.

The router therefore assumes the environment is frozen for the entire voyage — 17.4 days, in our own computed route. The problem statement explicitly requires dynamic recalculation as ice layers move. This is the gap that makes a forecast usable: a static router structurally cannot consume a lead-time forecast, because it has no notion of “the ice you will meet when you actually arrive.” The extension is a time-expanded graph — a mesh stack, one layer per forecast day, with edges (cell, t) → (neighbour, t + traveltime).

(c) Wave-added resistance is dead code

wave_resistance() is defined in three PolarRoute vessel classes and called from none of them. Resistance sums only wind and ice. Waves act solely as a binary accessibility cutoff, so there is no sea-state fuel penalty anywhere — including the Roaring Forties, where most of the fuel is actually burned. A latent bug compounds it: the dead method reads params["Beam"] while the constructor sets "beam". Note that a planned vessel subclass “with wave resistance” will silently do nothing unless model_resistance is also overridden.

(d) One logistics fact that must not go on a slide wrong

Indian expeditions are flagged off from Goa, but personnel fly to Cape Town and the sea leg starts there. Do not present “Goa → Antarctica by sea” as current practice — an MoES judge will know. The credible forward-looking framing is that GRSE signed an MoU with Kongsberg in June 2025 to build India's first indigenous Polar Research Vessel at Kolkata for NCPOR; when India has its own hull, the voyage genuinely starts from an Indian port.

§12Glossary

ADR — Architecture Decision Record. A short note stating a decision, its alternatives, and why.

AMSR2 — Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer 2, a Japanese satellite sensor giving ice concentration at 12.5 km.

Beset — a ship stuck fast in ice, unable to move under its own power. The failure mode this system exists to prevent.

Bergy bit / growler — small pieces of floating ice, roughly car- to house-sized. Not tracked by any dataset; the ice that actually holes a hull.

CDR — Climate Data Record. A long, consistently processed satellite record suitable for training.

CMEMS — Copernicus Marine Environment Monitoring Service. The EU's operational ocean forecasting service; our base field.

Conformal prediction — a distribution-free method that turns model outputs into prediction intervals with a measured coverage guarantee.

CRPS — Continuous Ranked Probability Score. Scores a probabilistic forecast, not just its mean.

Dijkstra — a shortest-path algorithm that is exact on a graph, as opposed to a heuristic search.

Exchangeability — the statistical assumption that calibration data and operational data come from the same distribution. Climate change breaks it.

GEBCO — General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans. The open global seabed-depth grid.

GLORYS12 — CMEMS's global ocean reanalysis at 1/12°, 1993–present. Our training background field.

IIEE — Integrated Ice Edge Error. Measures how wrong the position of the ice edge is, in km² — the navigationally meaningful metric.

Innovation — in data assimilation, the difference between a model's forecast and the observation. Our key input channel.

Iridium — a low-Earth-orbit satellite constellation, the only communications that genuinely covers the poles. Certus peaks around 704 kbps.

ISEA — Indian Scientific Expedition to Antarctica, run annually by NCPOR.

Lead (ice) — a navigable crack or channel of open water through pack ice.

Lead (time) — how many days ahead a forecast is valid for. Both senses appear in this project; context distinguishes them.

MIZ — Marginal Ice Zone. The transitional band between open water and solid pack ice, roughly 15–80 % concentration. Where ships sail and where forecasts are worst.

MoES — Ministry of Earth Sciences, Government of India.

NCPOR — National Centre for Polar and Ocean Research, Vasco da Gama, Goa. The problem statement's owner.

NSIDC — National Snow and Ice Data Center, USA. Source of our truth field.

Persistence — the baseline forecast “tomorrow will look like today.” Deceptively strong at short lead, and the baseline that matters.

Polar Class (PC1–PC7) — IACS structural ice classes for ships, PC1 strongest. Finnish-Swedish classes IA Super / IA / IB / IC are the lighter equivalents.

POLARIS — the IMO's Polar Operational Limit Assessment Risk Indexing System. Converts ice conditions plus ship ice class into a go / caution / avoid outcome.

Polynya — a persistent area of open water surrounded by sea ice.

Reanalysis — a retrospective reconstruction of past conditions using all observations, including ones from after the moment being reconstructed. Not a forecast.

Residual / bias correction — learning the error of an existing model rather than the quantity itself.

RIO / RIV — Risk Index Outcome and Risk Index Value, the two numbers POLARIS is built from.

RMSE — Root Mean Square Error. Lower is better.

SAR — Synthetic Aperture Radar. Sees through cloud and polar darkness.

SIC — Sea Ice Concentration, the fraction of a grid cell covered by ice, 0 to 1 (or 0–100 %). The central quantity in this project.

SIT — Sea Ice Thickness. Much harder to measure than concentration, and required for POLARIS.

U-Net — a convolutional neural network shaped like a U: it compresses an image, then expands it back, with skip connections preserving fine detail. Standard for image-to-image prediction.

Voyage pack — our compressed, signed, versioned bundle of everything the ship needs. Under 1 MB.

Wagner (2017) — the closed-form analytical iceberg drift model that is our physics baseline.

If any statement in this document cannot be traced to a measurement, a primary source, or a line of code, treat that as a defect and raise it. The project's entire credibility strategy rests on the difference between what we have measured and what we merely believe — and on saying which is which.
